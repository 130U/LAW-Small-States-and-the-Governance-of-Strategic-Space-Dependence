# Argument map

The Article's argument Part by Part: each claim, the authority or record it rests on, the strongest objection to it
and the response. Authorities are cited in full in the notes of the
[Article](../manuscript/small-states-and-the-governance-of-strategic-space-dependence.md).

## Thesis

Authorization, ownership, operational capability and political influence require separate analysis. Control over
space infrastructure is a bundle of separable entitlements, and the residual power to decide in a crisis follows
the chokepoint more than the title. The governing objective is informed, lawful and accountable choice over
important public functions, implemented through a standard of **accountable acceptance**: a procedural duty that
forbids uninformed dependence and leaves the choice to depend with the responsible authority.

## Part II. Defining the comparison and the mechanisms

| Claim | Authority | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Small state" is a transparent sampling screen | A population ceiling of ten million, after the Forum of Small States | Smallness is about power | Defining it by powerlessness would assume the conclusion |
| The cases are favorable to agency by design | Wealthy democracies with partnerships | They are unrepresentative | They can refute "dependence means surrender"; they do not describe typical outcomes for poorer states |
| Influence has intrinsic, derivative and collective sources, and network position with jurisdiction can coerce | Long; Farrell and Newman | Satellite services differ from finance and the internet | The extension to satellites is the Article's own |
| Practical autonomy is the capacity to make and implement a specified lawful public decision | Definition | Autonomy means self-sufficiency | Buying a capability can enlarge the decisions that are feasible, and ownership can leave essentials out of reach |
| Control is a bundle of five entitlements: permission, title, operation, interruption and priority, and voice | Hohfeld on legal relations; Honoré on the incidents of ownership | Sovereignty is a single thing a state has or lacks | Nothing requires one holder for all the incidents. The cases show them divided among a regulator, an owner, an operator and a supplier's government |
| The residual right in a crisis follows the chokepoint | Grossman and Hart; Hart and Moore on residual control under incomplete contracts | Title settles who decides | The party that holds a control segment, an export license or a ground station decides what the contract did not foresee, whatever the title says |
| Shelter theory explains entry into dependence and stops there | Thorhallsson | Small states simply buy protection | That account does not say how decisions are allocated inside the relationship, which is the part law can design |
| Rivalry raises the stakes without explaining every difficulty | The Cosmos 1408 debris; the KA-SAT attack | The arms race caused the national projects | Neither episode shows that; both show why continuity and third-party effects deserve scrutiny |

## Part III. What international law supplies and what it leaves open

| Provision | Supplies | Leaves open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer Space Treaty art. I | Equal freedom of exploration and use | A right to buy a particular foreign service or crisis capacity |
| Outer Space Treaty arts. III and IV | International law including the Charter; the ban on placing weapons of mass destruction | A comprehensive prohibition of military activity in orbit |
| Outer Space Treaty art. VI | State responsibility; authorization and continuing supervision | A domestic licensing system and the practical means to supervise |
| G.A. Res. 68/74 | Agreed guidance: authorization by a competent authority, continuing supervision, and supervision that survives a transfer of ownership or control in orbit | Dependence on a foreign input, which the recommendations do not name |
| Outer Space Treaty art. VIII | Registry jurisdiction and control; preserved ownership | Access to a foreign operator's systems; authority over hosted payloads |
| 22 C.F.R. § 121.1, Category XV(e)(17) | Platform and payload as different legal objects before any contract is signed: a commercial spacecraft stays subject to the EAR when it carries a hosted payload that stays on the Munitions List | Who can authorize each hosted payload's use |
| Outer Space Treaty arts. IX and XI | Due regard, consultation, qualified information | A veto, a universal disclosure regime, a continuity guarantee |
| Liability Convention | Absolute and fault liability for qualifying damage | Restoration of interrupted service |
| ITU Constitution arts. 44, 45, 48 | Equitable access; rules on harmful interference | Affordable terminals, dependable suppliers, crisis allocation |

## Part IV. Three arrangements and their limits

| Case | Claim | Record | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New Zealand | Launch permission implements national preferences while foreign expertise informs the decision | The 2016 Technology Safeguards Agreement; the 2017 Act and the 2019 payload principles | New Zealand simply follows United States policy | The national-interest refusal power and tailored conditions allocate responsibility domestically |
| | The lever is the condition and not the refusal | Statutory review (2022): seventy payload permits, none declined, ministerial conditions on every one | A regulator that never refuses does not regulate | Conditions are how the permission is exercised, and the review records it |
| | Discretion is hard to apply consistently without agreed risk criteria | Inspector-General's review (2021); the statutory review | The later review found compliant decisions | The two reviews asked different questions |
| | Permission before launch is narrower than supervision after it | The statutory review: remote-sensing products can be resold with "no guarantee about where they will end up" | Licensing means control of the mission | An official review states the distinction in terms |
| Norway | Dependence on foreign manufacture and launch coexisted with national ownership, command and an activated service | Space Norway's handover; the Ministry of Defence's activation | A launch announcement is not a capability | The record shows ownership, command and an activated Norwegian network |
| | The legislature financed the partnership on stated terms about who would control what | Innst. 330 S: military broadband under Norwegian control, no funds until binding customer agreements, ground station and operations center in Norway | Partnerships are simply cost-effective | The financing record shows conditions of the kind the Article proposes |
| | Shared procurement still needs public risk-bearing | Prop. 25 S: the equity commitment and overruns | A partnership announcement proves cost-effectiveness | It cannot |
| | Co-hosting civilian and military functions creates risk for others | Additional Protocol I arts. 51, 52, 57; the ICRC's 2024 observations | A military connection makes every component a target | The military-objective test, proportionality and precautions still apply |
| Luxembourg | Ownership and authorization do not ensure control without planning and information | Cour des comptes (2022): the envelope rose from EUR170 million to EUR309 million | Geopolitical dependence caused the failure | Comparable errors occur in large states and civilian projects |
| | The title did not include the capacity to operate | The audit: operation contracted to a third-party provider at EUR75 million, antennas on rented infrastructure at Redu in Belgium | Ownership is control | Ownership was acquired and the capacity to operate was bought separately |
| | The gap in information was flagged before commitment | Conseil d'État, opinion on the 2018 bill | The audit is hindsight | The legislature authorized the title without an account of the operation |

**What the comparison explains.** No case combines all the entitlements, and the one that decides a crisis, the
power to interrupt, is the one the public record leaves least defined. Where it can be located it sits with
whoever controls a necessary input. The remedy follows the decision at risk, and not the nationality of every
input.

| Entitlement | New Zealand (launch regulation) | Norway (ASBM) | Luxembourg (LUXEOSys) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Permission | Held, and exercised through conditions on every permit | Not at issue: the state owns the operator | Not at issue: a state program |
| Title | Not held: the payloads belong to foreign operators | Held: Space Norway owns the platforms | Held |
| Operation | Not held | Platform command held; the American and commercial payloads are separate | Contracted to a third-party provider, with antennas on rented infrastructure abroad |
| Interruption and priority | Before launch only | Not resolved by the published terms | Not documented in the audit |

## Part V. Participation and influence in multilateral governance

| Claim | Record | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A vote proves neither influence nor enforcement | G.A. Res. 77/41, adopted 155 to 9 with 9 abstentions | The resolution is a ban | It expresses restraint on one source of debris |
| Consensus protects small participants and lets persistent opposition block a product | The 2022 to 2023 open-ended working group adopted no report | Consensus equalizes | It equalizes the rule, and not the resources to sustain negotiation |
| Process burdens weigh on small delegations | New Zealand's 2023 statement; the consolidation of processes in decision 79/512 | New Zealand caused the consolidation | The record does not show causation |
| Agreement at one level coexists with disagreement at another | The 2024 report of the Group of Governmental Experts | An adopted report is a verdict | The question is what was agreed |
| Regional pooling allocates authority differently | ESA Convention: optional programs, one vote, industrial return linked to contributions | Pooling guarantees influence | It organizes shared capacity |

## Part VI. Safeguards for accountable choice

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Standard | Identify interruption powers, usable response rights and funded capacity, and justify material gaps before commitment ([checklist](proposal.md)) |
| Kind of duty | Procedural, as environmental review is: NEPA "does not mandate particular results" and prohibits uninformed, and not unwise, agency action (*Robertson v. Methow Valley Citizens Council*). Accountable acceptance leaves the choice to depend with the responsible authority and forbids making it uninformed |
| Authorization | Permitted activities are connected to information the regulator can obtain and assess, with budget and competence for continuing supervision |
| Essential services | Suspension grounds, notice, emergency consultation, operational information, disagreement procedures, transition |
| Where the terms come from | European financial regulation on outside technology providers (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 arts. 28, 30) and European defense procurement (Directive 2009/81/EC art. 23). The Article's claim is that their absence from an arrangement for an essential space function is something a named public decision maker has to explain |
| Public finance | A lifetime comparison of feasible alternatives, and material revisions to parliament while choices remain open |

| Objection | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Safeguards cost money and may deter suppliers | A duty to identify and explain is a burden on the buyer's own process. It imposes on the seller only what the buyer then chooses to negotiate, and requirements scale with the importance of the function |
| Redundancy can be illusory | Two contracts may share gateways or export jurisdictions, so independence has to be shown |
| Wealth, geography and alignment explain the results | The cases show how legal allocations accompany retained choices |
| A reliable ally needs no contract and a determined one ignores it | Clarification and review help in ordinary operations and limited disputes |
| Protective arrangements externalize costs | Domestic reform accompanies international restraint and support for states without technical delegations |

## Conclusion

A small state's relationship with foreign space infrastructure is a question about particular decisions: who holds
each one, what information it takes, and what capacity carries it out. Control is a bundle of entitlements, and the
one that matters most in a crisis follows the chokepoint.
